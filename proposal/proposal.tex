\section{Problem and Motivation}
Malware uses Domain Generation Algorithms (DGAs) to create many domains from a
shared seed, so infected hosts can reach the attacker without a fixed
address~\cite{plohmann2016dgarchive}. We detect DGAs from the domain name alone.
This is hard because families differ: some produce random strings
(\texttt{gqspwpsurdun.ru}, CryptoLocker) or hex hashes (Bamital), while others join
dictionary words (\texttt{kneemanualgirl.com}) and look legitimate.

\textbf{Question.} For a DGA/legitimate decision, is it better to train on the
DGA families and collapse the prediction (\emph{family-first}), or to train on
DGA/legitimate directly (\emph{binary})?

\textbf{Why it matters.} Name-based filters, e.g.\ in DNS resolvers, must judge a
query before any connection is made. If family-first wins, family labels are
worth collecting even when only a binary decision is needed; if not, binary
labels are enough.

\textbf{Goal.} For each model, the difference in macro-F1 between family-first
and binary on the same test set, over 5 seeds. Family-first counts as better if
its mean is higher by more than the standard deviation.

\section{Impact}
\textbf{If it succeeds.} Teams building name-based DGA filters learn whether
family labels are worth collecting. Per-family results show which DGAs
(random-string or dictionary-based) are missed.

\textbf{If it fails.} Families that neither setup separates from legitimate
domains, likely dictionary-based ones, need network features such as the
NXDOMAIN rate or query periodicity~\cite{antonakakis2012pleiades}. Knowing
which families these are is itself useful.

\textbf{Risks.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Unequal errors:} a false negative leaves a host talking to its
  attacker; a false positive blocks a real service. Since most DNS traffic is
  legitimate, even a small false-positive rate means many alarms.
  \item \emph{Data bias:} Tranco~\cite{lepochat2019tranco} favours popular
  sites, so unusual legitimate names (non-English, short, CDN hosts) may be
  flagged. Families with fewer than 300 domains are merged into one class, so we
  report no per-family results for them.
  \item \emph{Validity:} we test only families seen in training; new or
  adversarial DGAs may not be detected.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\citet{woodbridge2016lstm} trained a character-level LSTM on 30 DGA families: a
binary model reached an AUC of 0.9993, but a separate family model only 0.54
macro-F1 (0.96 when similar families were grouped). They never collapsed the
family model into a detector, which is the comparison we make.

\citet{gregorio2024incremental} classified 61 families with a character-level CNN
(average F1 84\%, some families at 0\%), but with no legitimate class. We add
that class and test whether family confusions still matter after collapsing.

\citet{torrealba2025deepdga} (DeepDGA, BiLSTM with word embeddings) exceed 0.97
precision on dictionary DGAs, where feature-based models stay below 0.89; they
use a bigger architecture, we test richer labels.

\citet{shahzad2021dga} found little difference between LSTM, BiLSTM and GRU
(AUC $\approx$ 0.98) for binary detection. Since the architecture barely
mattered, we change the labels instead; we also follow their 80/10/10 split.

\citet{dahal2019autoencoded} reached 88\% accuracy with autoencoded character
counts, falling to 78\% on unseen families; we test known families only, so this
drop marks the limit of our results.

\section{Data}
\textbf{Sources.} DGA domains come from Greg{\'o}rio and Cansian (Mendeley, 2025,
CC~BY~4.0~\cite{gregorio2025dataset}; 3{,}090{,}661 domains, 160 families, from
DGArchive~\cite{plohmann2016dgarchive}) and Givre (Kaggle, 2021, license not
stated, academic use~\cite{givre2021kaggle}; 80{,}000 domains, 7 families).
Legitimate domains come from the Tranco top 1M~\cite{lepochat2019tranco} (list
\texttt{64VVX}, \url{https://tranco-list.eu/list/64VVX}); the DGA datasets'
legitimate rows are not used.

\textbf{Size, input and target.} 1{,}999{,}335 domains: 999{,}664 legitimate and
999{,}671 DGA in 103 families (Fig.~\ref{fig:data}). The input is the full domain
name, TLD included; all features are derived from it. The targets are the binary
label and the family (\texttt{legit} or one of 103). DGA domains are longer (mean
18.4 vs.\ 13.7 characters), have higher character entropy (3.17 vs.\ 2.79 bits)
and more digits (15\% vs.\ 4\%). We use pandas~\cite{mckinney2010pandas} and
Matplotlib~\cite{hunter2007matplotlib}.

\textbf{Cleaning.} No missing values. We drop 330 domains found in both Tranco and
a DGA list (e.g.\ \texttt{pywolwnvd.biz}), 141 duplicates and 7 invalid strings,
and map Kaggle family names to Mendeley's.

\textbf{Balancing.} The 161 cleaned families range from 2 to 519{,}280 domains. We
keep all legitimate domains, merge the 59 families under 300 domains into
\texttt{other\_dga}, and cap each family at 29{,}120 so that DGA and legitimate are
equal in number. Sample weights give legitimate and DGA half each and, within DGA,
are proportional to $1/\sqrt{n_f}$ ($n_f$: family size; maximum ratio 9.9).

\textbf{Biases.} Tranco favours popular sites. 23 DGA families use a single TLD
(legitimate domains use 1{,}030), so the TLD alone separates some families. DGA
data cover only 2021 and late 2025.

\section{Proposed Models and Methods}
Every model is trained in two setups on the same data: \emph{binary}
(DGA/legitimate) and \emph{family-first} (\texttt{legit} or one of 103 families,
collapsed with $P(\text{DGA}) = 1 - P(\texttt{legit})$). Neural models are also
trained \emph{multi-task}~\cite{caruana1997multitask}: a shared encoder with a
binary and a family head whose losses are summed, so family labels shape the
representation without a collapse step.

\textbf{Model 0: majority class.} The floor every model must beat.

\textbf{Model 1: logistic regression}~\cite{pedregosa2011sklearn}. Fast and
interpretable, but linear: in the binary setup it draws one boundary around very
different families, while family-first gives one per family. If family labels
help anywhere, it should be here.

\textbf{Model 2: character-level CNN}~\cite{gregorio2024incremental}
(PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch}). Convolutions learn local patterns (hex runs,
vowel--consonant alternation, word pieces), which is where families differ. It
shows whether the gain from family labels shrinks as the model gets more flexible.

\textbf{Model 3: character-level Transformer}~\cite{vaswani2017attention}.
Self-attention relates characters across the whole name, which suits dictionary
DGAs, the hardest case~\cite{torrealba2025deepdga}, built from whole words. We
also test whether CNN or Transformer embeddings fed to Model~1 match the full
neural models.

\section{Evaluation}
\textbf{Metrics} (on the DGA/legitimate decision unless stated).
\emph{Macro-F1} is the main metric: it averages both classes' F1, so favouring one
class does not pay, and~\citet{woodbridge2016lstm} use it. \emph{DGA precision and
recall} capture the unequal error costs (Section~2). \emph{ROC-AUC} is
threshold-free and allows comparison with published work. \emph{Recall per
family} shows which families, e.g.\ dictionary-based ones, are missed. Accuracy
alone is not used, as it hides which errors are made.

\textbf{Data split.} One fixed 80/10/10 train/validation/test split, stratified by
family and shared by all models and setups. Hyperparameters are tuned on
validation; the test set is used once. Results are mean $\pm$ standard deviation
over 5 seeds.

\textbf{Comparisons.} \emph{Baseline:} Model~0 is the floor, and each model's
binary setup is the reference for family-first and multi-task. \emph{Ablations:}
the three setups per model, and each model with and without the TLD, since the TLD
alone separates some families. \emph{Published results:} AUC of
0.9993~\cite{woodbridge2016lstm} and $\approx$0.98~\cite{shahzad2021dga}, family
F1 of 84\%~\cite{gregorio2024incremental}; on other data, so only indicative.
